The International Autonomous Karting Series (IAKS) challenges student teams to build fully autonomous go-karts that race on real tracks without human input. Our team at Purdue won 1st place. Here's how we did it.

## The Problem

A go-kart is not a research platform. There's no ROS-friendly sensor mount, no power distribution board, no clean CAN bus. You get a steel frame, a gas engine, and a rulebook. Everything else — perception, planning, control, power — you build yourself.

## System Architecture

We split the system into three layers:

**Perception** — A forward-facing camera feeds a custom YOLOv8 model trained on track boundary images. A 2D LiDAR provides obstacle detection and distance estimation. The two sensors feed a fusion pipeline that outputs a cleaned occupancy grid at ~30 Hz.

**Planning** — A pure-pursuit path planner runs on the occupancy grid. We precompute a racing line from a map of the track, then the planner generates throttle and steering commands to follow it while respecting obstacle margins.

**Control** — Throttle and steering are actuated via PWM signals to a servo and an ESC. We ran a PID loop for steering that we tuned across two full practice days on-track.

## The Hard Parts

**Latency budget.** The perception pipeline had to run end-to-end in under 33 ms to maintain 30 Hz control. We profiled YOLO inference heavily and switched from Python to a TensorRT-optimized C++ inference server. That cut latency from 80 ms to 22 ms.

**Track generalization.** Our model was trained on our own track but had to generalize to the competition track, which we'd never seen. We used aggressive augmentation — brightness shifts, rotations, synthetic shadows — to build robustness into the model rather than tuning at the venue.

**Power and heat.** The onboard NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX runs hot under full load. We designed a custom mounting bracket with a heatsink and forced-air cooling to keep it under 70°C on long runs.

## Race Day

The kart ran three full laps without intervention at speeds up to 40 km/h. The hardest moment came in practice: a sensor dropout caused a spin-out, traced back to a single loose USB cable. The fix was zip-tying every cable to the frame. Simple problems, expensive lessons.

## What I'd Do Differently

The pure-pursuit planner reacts slowly to obstacles appearing at high speed. A model-predictive control (MPC) approach would give better look-ahead. I'd also move the sensor fusion layer onto an FPGA for deterministic latency instead of relying on Linux scheduling.
